# Expert brief: reading an anaerobic digester's record

This brief is the background a practitioner brings to a digester record. It is written
from the published literature, cited in brackets, and it says nothing about the cases you
will see: not which causes are present, not how large they are, not when they begin. Use
it to reason about what a candidate cause would do to the channels and the balances; let
the record decide.

## 1. The channels and how fast they respond

A digester record carries a handful of channels, and they answer to a disturbance at
different speeds (Switzenbaum et al., 1990; Boe et al., 2010; Madsen et al., 2011):

- **Gas flow** reports the rate of methanogenesis plus carbon dioxide release. It answers
  a change of load or of activity within hours, because the gas phase holds little.
  Volumes depend on temperature, pressure and water vapour, so any comparison needs a
  stated reference condition and a dry basis; unnormalised volumes carry errors of the
  same order as real process changes (Walker et al., 2009; Strömberg et al., 2014).
- **Methane fraction** of the gas moves when the balance between acid production and
  methane production shifts: more carbon leaving as carbon dioxide lowers it. It answers
  within hours, ahead of the liquid-phase indicators (Boe et al., 2010; Molina et al.,
  2009). Hydrogen and carbon monoxide in the gas rise early in an overload (Hickey and
  Switzenbaum, 1991; Björnsson et al., 2000), where they are measured.
- **Volatile fatty acids (VFA)** are the intermediates between hydrolysis and
  methanogenesis. Acetate and propionate accumulate when their consumers fall behind;
  propionate responds later than acetate and clears later, which makes it the more
  persistent indicator (Ahring et al., 1995; Pind et al., 2003; Nielsen et al., 2007).
  The propionate-to-acetate ratio was proposed as a failure predictor by Hill et al.
  (1987).
- **Alkalinity** is the buffer, in practice mainly bicarbonate. A VFA rise consumes
  bicarbonate before it moves the pH, so alkalinity falls first. The ratio of
  intermediate to partial alkalinity (Ripley et al., 1986) and the VFA-to-alkalinity
  ratio (FOS/TAC in the German operating literature; Nordmann, 1977) were designed to
  read that consumption before the pH does.
- **pH** is set by the bicarbonate and carbon dioxide equilibrium together with the VFA
  and ammonia present (Batstone et al., 2002). In a well-buffered digester it is the last
  channel to move under acid accumulation and the first to move under a change of the
  gas-phase carbon dioxide, so a pH excursion with steady alkalinity and VFA should be
  read against the gas composition (Speece, 1996).
- **Total ammonia nitrogen (TAN)** comes from the deamination of protein in the feed. It
  follows the feed nitrogen over the hydraulic residence time and is not a fast
  indicator; its interest is the inhibitory free-ammonia fraction, which rises with pH
  and temperature (Hansen et al., 1998).
- **Temperature** is an operating condition rather than a response. Every rate constant
  and every dissociation equilibrium in the digester depends on it (Batstone et al.,
  2002), so a temperature channel is checked before any biological explanation is offered
  for a change that coincides with it.

The order in which the channels respond to an acid-side disturbance, in the literature's
account, is gas composition and hydrogen first, then VFA, then alkalinity, then pH
(Ahring et al., 1995; Switzenbaum et al., 1990; Boe et al., 2010). A candidate cause is
tested against that order as much as against the size of the change.

## 2. Process upsets and how they present

**Organic overload.** Feeding faster than the methanogens can follow. Gas flow rises with
the load at first, the methane fraction falls as carbon dioxide rises, acetate then
propionate accumulate, alkalinity is consumed, and the pH falls last; when it does,
methanogenesis is inhibited and the gas flow falls below where it started (Ahring et al.,
1995; Hill et al., 1987; Pind et al., 2003; Boe et al., 2010). A step of load produces a
VFA transient that clears if the step is within capacity; a sustained accumulation is the
signature of an overload proper (Pind et al., 2003; Nielsen et al., 2007).

**Underfeeding.** A starved digester is a calm one: gas flow falls in proportion to the
load, the methane fraction holds or rises a little, VFA stay low and the pH steady
(Speece, 1996). The record shows less of everything and no stress marker.

**Hydraulic overload and washout.** The slowest-growing populations, the propionate
oxidisers and the acetoclastic methanogens, set the minimum retention time. Below it,
their numbers decline over days to weeks, propionate climbs progressively, methane per
unit of feed falls, and recovery is slow because the lost populations must regrow
(Pavlostathis and Giraldo-Gomez, 1991; Speece, 1996). Dilute feed at high flow produces
the same pattern with a low load, which separates it from an organic overload.

**Ammonia inhibition.** Protein-rich feeds release ammonia. The toxic species is the
undissociated free ammonia, whose share of the TAN rises with pH and with temperature
(Hansen et al., 1998; Angelidaki and Ahring, 1993; Rajagopal et al., 2013). The
presentation differs from an acid-side upset: an "inhibited steady state" with acetate
elevated, methane yield per unit of feed reduced, and the pH held high rather than
low, because ammonia adds alkalinity (Angelidaki and Ahring, 1993; Hansen et al., 1998;
Nielsen and Angelidaki, 2008). The VFA-to-alkalinity ratios therefore understate it, and
the telling channels are TAN, the methane yield and the temperature that sets the free
fraction. Populations acclimate over time, so thresholds quoted from one plant do not
transfer to another (Chen et al., 2008).

**Acidification.** When the alkalinity is exhausted the pH drops, and the drop itself
inhibits the methanogens, which lets the acids accumulate faster: the end state of an
uncorrected overload (Speece, 1996; Chen et al., 2008). Low pH with low alkalinity and
high VFA together is that state; low pH with normal alkalinity and VFA is something else,
and the instrument is the first suspect.

**Sulfide.** Sulfate in the feed lets sulfate-reducing bacteria compete with the
methanogens for acetate and hydrogen and produce sulfide, whose undissociated form is
toxic and pH-dependent. The gas carries hydrogen sulfide, and methane per unit of feed
falls without an acid-side signature (Koster et al., 1986; Chen et al., 2008).

**Temperature shifts.** A change of operating temperature moves the rates and the
equilibria at once: a transient VFA rise and a gas change follow a step, and recovery
takes time; thermophilic operation is the more sensitive (Ahring et al., 2001; Chen et
al., 2008). A temperature record that moves with the gas record explains the gas record.

**Hydrolysis limitation.** For particulate feeds, hydrolysis is the rate-limiting step
(Pavlostathis and Giraldo-Gomez, 1991; Vavilin et al., 2008). Its signature is a methane
yield below the feed's potential with no stress marker at all: VFA low, pH steady,
alkalinity intact. It is a kinetic feature of the feed and the retention time, not an
upset, and a longer retention time raises the yield.

**Mixing.** Departures from ideal mixing leave part of the volume inactive or let feed
short-circuit to the outlet. The effect is a yield that falls as the load rises and a
residual that tracks the load rather than the time (Lindmark et al., 2014; Batstone et
al., 2002, on the ideal-mixing assumption).

## 3. Feed characterisation errors: what the balances see and what the kinetics see

A model's input is the feed's quantity and its composition, and errors in the two show
differently (Kleerebezem and van Loosdrecht, 2006; Zaher et al., 2009; Nopens et al.,
2009).

**Quantity errors** (a mass or volume misreported, a load that was fed but not logged)
scale every output together. The COD balance, which compares the COD fed with the COD
leaving as methane, in the effluent and accumulated in the reactor, closes when the feed
COD is right whatever the biology does, because kinetics decide where the COD goes, not
how much there is. A closure gap that scales with the load, or that appears when
deliveries appear, points to the feed quantity or to an unlogged input, not to the
kinetics (Meijer et al., 2002; Puig et al., 2008; Rieger et al., 2010).

**Composition errors** change the ratios. A moisture or solids error scales the COD and
the nitrogen fed together, so both balances open by the same proportion. A protein
content error moves the nitrogen balance without a methane counterpart: effluent TAN plus
organic nitrogen stops matching the nitrogen fed, while the COD closes (Kleerebezem and
van Loosdrecht, 2006; Rieger et al., 2010). A biodegradability error, the fraction of the
COD that can be converted, opens the COD balance in one direction only, methane short of
the feed, with the effluent carrying the difference as unconverted solids (Sötemann et
al., 2005; Ekama et al., 2006).

**What the kinetics see.** A change of kinetic parameters alters the dynamics: how fast
the gas answers a step of load, how large the VFA transient is, how long recovery takes.
It does not alter the long-run distribution of the COD. A composition error alters the
long-run yield. When a fit is free to move the kinetic and yield parameters, it will
absorb an input error into them and report a better fit, because routine data identify
these parameters weakly (Dochain and Vanrolleghem, 2001; Donoso-Bravo et al., 2011;
Lauwers et al., 2013). The balances are therefore checked before the kinetics are moved.

## 4. Instruments: how they fail, and how a failure shows

Instrument faults on treatment plants fall into a small number of kinds (Rieger et al.,
2003; Thomann et al., 2002; Olsson et al., 2005; Vanrolleghem and Lee, 2003):

- a **drifting zero or span**: a pH electrode ageing or fouling, a reference junction
  clogging, a gas meter wearing; the signal walks away from the truth over days to
  weeks;
- a **stuck or flat signal**: a frozen output, a lost communication link, a fouled
  electrode that no longer responds; the variance vanishes while the plant goes on
  varying;
- a **changed factor**: a gas meter re-spanned after maintenance, a wrong range, a
  volume reported at the wrong reference condition (Walker et al., 2009); the signal
  keeps the shape of the truth at the wrong size;
- **noise, spikes, gaps and timestamp errors**, which corrupt single values rather than
  the series (Rieger et al., 2010);
- for grab-sampled quantities such as VFA, alkalinity and TAN, **sampling and handling
  errors** and a reporting lag between the sample and its result (Madsen et al., 2011).

The distinguishing property of an instrument fault is that it breaks the relations that
physics and chemistry impose between the channels while the process itself remains
consistent (Thomann et al., 2002; Rieger et al., 2010; Olsson et al., 2005):

- pH, alkalinity, VFA and the carbon dioxide fraction of the gas are bound together by
  the carbonate equilibrium (Batstone et al., 2002; Ripley et al., 1986); a pH that
  departs while the other three agree with each other implicates the electrode;
- gas flow is bound to the COD fed and to the methane fraction through the COD balance;
  a flow that departs from that balance by a fixed proportion, from a date, unchanging
  through the process events that follow, implicates the meter, since no process cause
  holds a fixed proportion through varying load;
- a channel whose variance vanishes while the coupled channels vary is not measuring;
- TAN is bound to the feed nitrogen over the residence time; a TAN step without a feed
  change and without the alkalinity and pH response that ammonia brings implicates the
  analysis rather than the digester.

A process upset moves the coupled channels together, in the order of §1; an instrument
fault moves one channel against the rest. Maintenance and calibration events in the
operator's notes are the natural anchor for a change of an instrument's behaviour, and
a change that coincides with one is read that way first.

## 5. What a mass balance can and cannot decide

A balance over a window compares what entered with what left and what accumulated
(Meijer et al., 2002; Puig et al., 2008; Rieger et al., 2010). It can:

- detect an inconsistency among the measured flows and concentrations, which is the
  starting point of every data-quality investigation on a plant;
- test the feed nitrogen, because nitrogen is conserved through digestion: what is fed
  as protein leaves as ammonia and organic nitrogen;
- test the feed COD and quantity against the methane produced, given normalised gas
  volumes and an effluent COD;
- localise a gap in time: a step in the closure at a date points to a change of an
  input or an instrument at that date, and a gap that scales with the load points to a
  quantity.

It cannot:

- say which of the flows in an open balance is the wrong one, without a third,
  independent measurement of the same quantity;
- decide the kinetics: a closed balance is consistent with any rate constants, and an
  open one says nothing about them;
- resolve anything within a window shorter than the residence time, where the
  accumulation term dominates and is itself uncertain (Meijer et al., 2002);
- check what is not measured: the biodegradable fraction, an unmetered side stream, the
  gas lost to leaks.

The balance is the arbiter between the feed and the instruments on one side and the
biology on the other; it is not an arbiter among rate constants.

## 6. Background misfit versus a cause

Every model misfits its plant. ADM1 lumps populations, uses a single hydrolysis rate per
substrate, assumes ideal mixing and a fixed feed composition, and carries default
parameters from other plants (Batstone et al., 2002; Rosén and Jeppsson, 2006; Jeppsson
et al., 2007). The residual pattern that these simplifications leave is present from the
first day, spread across the channels, and correlated with the load and the feed
composition. It is what parameter estimation absorbs, and re-fitting kinetics to it
improves the fit without meaning anything about the plant (Donoso-Bravo et al., 2011;
Lauwers et al., 2013).

A cause adds a pattern that the structure does not predict: a change at a time, one
channel leaving the others, a link to a particular feed batch, a balance that opens. The
test of a candidate cause is to ask what it would do to each channel and each balance,
in what order, and whether the record shows that and the background does not. A
candidate that only re-describes the background, a misfit everywhere of the same shape
as before, is not supported by it.

Two cautions from the identifiability literature (Dochain and Vanrolleghem, 2001;
Donoso-Bravo et al., 2011): a fitted parameter that runs to a bound is telling you that
the data do not locate it, not that the plant has moved; and a fit that improves when
several parameters move together is telling you that the data locate a combination, not
the parameters.
